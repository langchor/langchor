Langchor.

Language Anchoring Simulator · First convergence in bilingual conversation

Language anchoring is the process by which interlocutors converge on a shared language during a multilingual interaction. Langchor models the first matching response to a speaker who consistently uses one language—not whether that choice later stabilizes.

What the model does — and does not — measure

✓ Measures

  • The probability that B first uses the anchor's language by opportunity n.
  • Probability of a Catalan response at a specified opportunity.
  • Waiting time to the first match, under stated assumptions.

✕ Does not measure

  • Long-term language stabilization or habit formation.
  • Why a speaker accommodates (identity, attitudes, context).
  • Whether B continues using Catalan after the first match.
CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

Language anchoring begins with a person

In this exploratory scenario, the anchor is an interlocutor who consistently maintains one language. The model tracks the other interlocutor's first use of that language.

First convergence ≠ stabilization

One matching response is the first convergence event. The model deliberately does not infer a stable language choice from that event. Persistence after first convergence is a separate empirical question.

The fixed-anchor assumption describes a particular interactional scenario; it is not a claim that real speakers never switch languages in other settings.

Mathematical models

Why use Bernoulli models?

We are interested in the first time Speaker B chooses the anchor speaker’s language. Each response can therefore be coded as a binary event: match or no match. A Bernoulli trial is a simple mathematical description of such a two-outcome opportunity. Repeating these opportunities lets us calculate the chance that a first match has occurred by response n.

Constant model: the chance of matching remains the same on every opportunity. This provides a transparent baseline: cumulative success can rise even when the speaker’s underlying probability never changes. Variable model: the chance of matching can differ across opportunities. This allows us to explore hypothetical accommodation, but Bernoulli theory itself does not explain why the chance changes or determine the size of that change.

These are illustrative probability models of first convergence, not empirically calibrated predictions of real bilingual conversations. They do not model language stabilization.

Constant probability · geometric waiting time

F(n) = P(T ≤ n) = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ

Here p is the fixed probability that B uses Catalan on each response. The formula assumes independent opportunities with the same p. Even when p stays fixed, F(n) increases as more opportunities accumulate.

Variable probability · discrete-time first-event hazard

F(n) = 1 − ∏ᵢ₌₁ⁿ (1 − pᵢ)

Here pᵢ is the conditional probability of first convergence at opportunity i given no earlier convergence. The illustrative slider rule is pᵢ = clamp(p₁ + (i−1)Δ, 0, 1). It is not a validated psychological mechanism or a Bayesian update.

Three different quantities

Per-opportunity probability: p or pᵢ. Cumulative probability: P(T ≤ n). Exact first-event probability: P(T = n) = pₙ ∏ⱼ₌₁ⁿ⁻¹ (1 − pⱼ).

Scope and limitations

n counts opportunities for B to respond, not necessarily all conversational turns. With fixed p, the expected waiting time is 1/p when p > 0. With variable p, the expected waiting time depends on the full sequence of conditional probabilities and can be infinite. The simulation reports a truncated numerical estimate only when a finite expectation is not analytically guaranteed.

Model parameters are user-defined scenarios, not inferred empirical estimates. Actual accommodation may depend on interactional history and other contextual variables. A rising cumulative curve alone is not evidence of accommodation.

SCIENTIFIC CONTEXT

Theoretical background & references

The references below motivate the binary-event mathematical framework and the linguistic question of conversational alignment and accommodation. They do not establish that a linear increment is a validated psychological law or that these equations predict a particular bilingual pair.

Probability foundations

  1. Bernoulli, J. (1713). Ars conjectandi. Historical foundation of probability theory. Digitized historical work (DOI) ↗. The DOI identifies a digitized edition, not a modern empirical study of language anchoring.
  2. Hald, A. (1990). James Bernoulli and Ars Conjectandi, 1713. In A History of Probability and Statistics and Their Applications before 1750. DOI ↗.

Conversational alignment & language accommodation

  1. Pickering, M. J., & Garrod, S. (2004). Toward a mechanistic psychology of dialogue. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 27, 169–226. DOI ↗. Provides a broader theoretical account of alignment during dialogue.
  2. Bawa, A., Choudhury, M., & Bali, K. (2018). Accommodation of conversational code-choice. Proceedings of the Third Workshop on Computational Approaches to Linguistic Code-Switching, 82–91. DOI ↗. Empirical motivation for studying coordination of bilingual language choice; not a validation of Langchor's particular adjustment rule.
Interpretive boundary: the geometric waiting-time expression follows from repeated fixed-probability Bernoulli opportunities; the varying-probability product is a discrete first-event model. Their application to language choice is an explicit modelling proposal, not a formula derived from the cited accommodation studies.
