Langchor.

Language Anchoring Simulator · First convergence in bilingual conversation

Language anchoring is the process by which interlocutors converge on a shared language during a multilingual interaction. Langchor models the first matching response to a speaker who consistently uses one language—not whether that choice later stabilizes.

CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK

Language anchoring begins with a person

In this exploratory scenario, the anchor is an interlocutor who consistently maintains one language. The model tracks the other interlocutor's first use of that language.

First convergence ≠ stabilization

One matching response is the first convergence event. The model deliberately does not infer a stable language choice from that event. Persistence after first convergence is a separate empirical question.

The fixed-anchor assumption describes a particular interactional scenario; it is not a claim that real speakers never switch languages in other settings.

Mathematical models

Why use Bernoulli models?

We are interested in the first time Speaker B chooses the anchor speaker’s language. Each response can therefore be coded as a binary event: match or no match. A Bernoulli trial is a simple mathematical description of such a two-outcome opportunity. Repeating these opportunities lets us calculate the chance that a first match has occurred by response n.

Constant model: the chance of matching remains the same on every opportunity. This provides a transparent baseline: cumulative success can rise even when the speaker’s underlying probability never changes. Variable model: the chance of matching can differ across opportunities. This allows us to explore hypothetical accommodation, but Bernoulli theory itself does not explain why the chance changes or determine the size of that change.

These are illustrative probability models of first convergence, not empirically calibrated predictions of real bilingual conversations. They do not model language stabilization.

Constant probability · geometric waiting time

F(n) = P(T ≤ n) = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ

Here p is the fixed probability that B uses Catalan on each response. The formula assumes independent opportunities with the same p. Even when p stays fixed, F(n) increases as more opportunities accumulate.

Variable probability · discrete-time first-event hazard

F(n) = 1 − ∏ᵢ₌₁ⁿ (1 − pᵢ)

In discrete-time event-history terminology, pᵢ is a conditional event probability (hazard): the probability of first convergence at opportunity i given no earlier convergence. The product is mathematically valid for these conditional probabilities; it does not require assuming that the unconditional outcomes are independent. The increment Δ is chosen by the user, not estimated by the software. A positive value may represent a hypothesis of increasing linguistic accommodation, not evidence of accommodation. The 0–1 cap is mathematical bookkeeping and may create an unrealistic 100% probability. The illustrative slider rule is pᵢ = clamp(p₁ + (i−1)Δ, 0, 1). It is not a validated psychological mechanism or a Bayesian update.

Worked example: what does +5 pp mean?

With p₁ = 0.20 and Δ = +0.05, the successive conditional probabilities are 0.20, 0.25 and 0.30. The probability of a first match by opportunity 3 is 1 − (0.80 × 0.75 × 0.70) = 58%. This is not the same as the 30% chance on opportunity 3, conditional on two prior nonmatches. The +5 pp is a researcher-selected scenario, not a measured effect size.

Three different quantities

Per-opportunity probability: p or pᵢ. Cumulative probability: P(T ≤ n). Exact first-event probability: P(T = n) = pₙ ∏ⱼ₌₁ⁿ⁻¹ (1 − pⱼ).

Analogies from event-history research

Many biological and psychological questions concern when an event first occurs: for example, first diagnosis, onset of a behavior, or a transition between social states. Discrete-time event-history methods allow the conditional probability of such an event to be constant or vary across observation periods. This is an analogy of mathematical structure, not evidence that any particular diagnosis or behavior follows Langchor’s linear rule. See Allison (1982), DOI: 10.2307/270718. A geometric waiting-time model is appropriate only under a fixed probability per independent opportunity.

Scope and limitations

n counts opportunities for B to respond, not necessarily all conversational turns. With fixed p, the expected waiting time is 1/p when p > 0. With variable p, the expected waiting time depends on the full sequence of conditional probabilities and can be infinite. The simulation reports a truncated numerical estimate only when a finite expectation is not analytically guaranteed.

Model parameters are user-defined scenarios, not inferred empirical estimates. Actual accommodation may depend on interactional history and other contextual variables. A rising cumulative curve alone is not evidence of accommodation.

What the model does — and does not — measure

✓ Measures

  • The probability that B first uses the anchor's language by opportunity n.
  • Probability of a Catalan response at a specified opportunity.
  • Waiting time to the first match, under stated assumptions.

✕ Does not measure

  • Long-term language stabilization or habit formation.
  • Why a speaker accommodates (identity, attitudes, context).
  • Whether B continues using Catalan after the first match.

SOCIOLINGUISTIC APPLICATIONS · v0.6

Linguistic assertiveness & language maintenance

Linguistic submission is described by Tallers per la Llengua as the habitual tendency to switch to a socially dominant language. Linguistic assertiveness, by contrast, is the ability to continue speaking one's chosen language comfortably even when another language is used by the interlocutor. These are sociolinguistic descriptions of practices, not personality diagnoses.

In Catalan-speaking territories, a Catalan speaker may switch to Spanish automatically, including in interactions where mutual understanding in Catalan might have been possible. Workshops aim to make this choice more conscious and to provide respectful strategies for maintaining Catalan. This concern also applies to other minoritized languages, but the local circumstances and linguistic rights differ.

Three ideas that should not be confused

Maintaining one's language

Continuing in Catalan while listening to Spanish, when communication is possible. This does not require the other person to switch languages.

Inviting accommodation

Politely encouraging the other person to try Catalan, while accepting their linguistic autonomy and checking comprehension. This is an additional strategy, not a requirement for assertiveness.

Relation to the voice simulator: both agent modes maintain Catalan. The non-assertive mode means no explicit invitation to switch, not the “linguistic submission” described by Tallers per la Llengua. The assertive mode adds occasional respectful encouragement. Both can exemplify language maintenance. The Bernoulli curves do not measure workshop effectiveness or language vitality.

What happens in an assertiveness workshop?

  1. Recognize habits: identify situations in which speakers automatically change language and reflect on why.
  2. Examine assumptions: distinguish actual comprehension difficulties from untested expectations or social pressure.
  3. Practise responses: role-play encounters and rehearse calm, courteous ways to maintain Catalan.
  4. Negotiate respectfully: check understanding, avoid shaming, and adapt when communication or accessibility requires it.
  5. Reflect on language vitality: consider how everyday interactions affect opportunities to use and learn a minoritized language.

These are practical activities consistent with the published workshop descriptions, not a standardized protocol or a claim of demonstrated efficacy. The source highlights the role of mutual comprehension and the social and legal context of Catalan; comprehension should always be assessed in the actual interaction rather than assumed for every speaker.

Theoretical background & references
